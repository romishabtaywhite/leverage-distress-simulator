01

The question

Between 2020 and 2026, the benchmark rate underlying most corporate floating-rate debt went from near zero to over 5% and back down again. Companies that had loaded up on floating-rate loans — common in private-equity-backed capital structures — saw their interest bills swing dramatically without their underlying business changing at all.

This project asks two linked questions: how did that rate cycle actually move the needle on real companies' interest coverage, covenant headroom, and leverage — and can the simulated stress signal from that mechanism predict financial distress better than a static, backward-looking leverage ratio alone?

03

How it was built

Built incrementally, finance concepts explained from first principles before any code — each phase tested on real data before moving to the next.

01

Data infrastructure

A SQLite schema (companies, debt tranches, covenant terms, rate series, simulation results) fed by two live, free, public data pipelines: SEC EDGAR's XBRL API for real disclosed financials, and FRED for SOFR and credit spread history back to 2018.

  • edgar_client.py — CIK lookup, XBRL concept extraction, SEC fair-access rate limiting
  • fred_client.py — SOFR, Fed Funds, ICE BofA HY/BB/Single-B spread indices
02

The interest & covenant engine

A small set of composable functions: floating- and fixed-rate interest calculation, summed across a whole company's real tranches, tested against real disclosed covenant thresholds — including correctly recognizing when a covenant is historical and no longer active, not just most-recent.

  • engine/interest.py — floating_rate_interest(), fixed_rate_interest()
  • engine/company_interest.py — total_quarterly_interest(), get_total_debt_balance()
  • engine/ebitda.py — EBITDA proxy + Adjusted EBITDA with impairment add-backs
03

Historical replay

Running the engine across every real quarter since a company's current capital structure actually came into existence — not further back, since running a structure before it existed produces numbers that look real but aren't.

04

Equity returns

Enterprise Value = EBITDA × multiple; Equity Value = EV − Debt. Since debt stays fixed while EBITDA moves, all growth concentrates into the equity slice — Diebold's real 17.5% EBITDA growth became a 3x-amplified 51.9% equity return under an illustrative multiple. This amplification mechanism is the entire reason leverage exists in PE.

  • engine/equity.py — enterprise_value(), equity_value(), moic(), irr_approx()
05

Rate scenarios (Phase 3)

Deterministic shocks (±100 to +600bps) first, then a mean-reverting rate model calibrated directly from real historical SOFR via linear regression, simulated 500 times per company, 2 years ahead — turning "what happened" into "what could happen, and how likely."

  • engine/scenarios.py — calibrate_ou(), simulate_paths()
  • Results persisted to rate_scenarios / scenario_rate_paths / simulation_results tables

07

Can any of this predict distress?

The whole point of the simulated breach-probability feature is to beat static ratios at predicting real-world distress. Testing that honestly meant confronting a hard truth first: four labeled companies isn't enough to train anything meaningful.

A classifier "trained" on 4 examples doesn't learn a pattern — it memorizes 4 points. So before building anything, the dataset had to grow. 24 more real companies were researched — a mix of well-documented Chapter 11 filings (Hertz, WeWork, Rite Aid, Big Lots, Joann, 23andMe, Chesapeake Energy, Frontier Communications, Avaya, Revlon, Yellow Corporation, J.C. Penney) and heavily-levered survivors that haven't defaulted (Charter, Carnival, American Airlines, United, Occidental, Clear Channel Outdoor, Avantor, Herbalife, Kraft Heinz, Restaurant Brands, EchoStar, Anywhere Real Estate) — pulled via standardized SEC EDGAR tags rather than the deep tranche-level research done for the four core cases.

Of 28 companies attempted, 14 came through with complete, trustworthy data — 5 distressed, 9 not. The rest were excluded for real, stated reasons: several distressed companies had genuinely negative EBITDA in their bankruptcy year (a real signal, not a gap to paper over), a few used debt-tag conventions this pipeline couldn't resolve, and one (J.C. Penney) simply predates this API's coverage entirely.

The honest finding The model's accuracy exactly matches "always guess the majority class" — it hasn't demonstrated it beats the trivial baseline. Its AUC of 0.422 is below 0.5, meaning its predicted probabilities actually rank distressed and non-distressed companies worse than random guessing, driven specifically by PRTYQ — at 20.55x leverage, by far the most extreme point in the dataset, the model predicted only a 0.7% chance of distress. A logistic regression fit on 13 points ranging roughly 2.5x–10.5x has no reliable way to extrapolate to something nearly double the next-highest value: a textbook small-sample extrapolation failure, not a bug.

A permutation test (2,000 random label reshuffles, full LOOCV rerun each time) confirms it statistically: 27.8% of random reshuffles matched or beat the real accuracy (p=0.278), and 57.1% matched or beat the real AUC (p=0.571). By the conventional bar, this result is not distinguishable from chance at n=14. That's not a failure to bury — it's a rigorous, honestly-earned finding that directly makes the case for simulation-based features over correlational curve-fitting on a small sample.

DBD's miss tells a different story: its current ratios (3.38x leverage, 2.60x coverage) look genuinely healthy — because they are, today, after a real restructuring fixed the problem. The label "distressed" reflects a resolved past event; the features describe a recovered present. That mismatch isn't a modeling error, it's a structural limit of using single-snapshot ratios against historical labels.

Considered and rejected The tempting next step would be generating thousands of synthetic training rows from the 500-path stochastic simulation. Rejected: within one company's simulation, leverage and spread are held fixed — only the rate varies — so "coverage ratio" there is just a deterministic arithmetic function of the rate we already coded explicitly (interest = balance × (rate + spread)). Training a flexible model to rediscover a formula it was given isn't learning anything new; it's expensive curve-fitting on our own arithmetic. Better to not do it, and better still to say so.

How much leverage is too much — for this specific company

A genuinely new question, not a restatement of anything above: holding each company's real EBITDA and real floating-rate spread fixed, what if it had been levered at a different multiple — combined with calibrated rate uncertainty? Nine leverage levels × 500 simulated paths × 3 companies with real floating-rate exposure (Party City excluded — zero floating debt, so this question doesn't meaningfully apply to it).

Breakeven leverage is a genuinely company-specific number — it comes directly from a company's own real cash-generating capacity and real cost of debt, not a generic "6x is risky" industry rule of thumb. DBD and BHC, structurally similar in spread, land within half a turn of each other (~4.5x, ~4.9x); CYH's far lower spread pushes its threshold out to ~8.5x. That's the differentiator the whole project was built around, made concrete.

09

What broke, and what that revealed

Six genuine data-quality issues across the project, caught and fixed rather than smoothed over — each one a real lesson about working with disclosed financial data, not a polished demo that just happened to work.

Fresh-start accounting created fake "annual" figures around Diebold's bankruptcy

Companies emerging from Chapter 11 file a partial-year "Predecessor" stub and a partial-year "Successor" stub — both tagged as if they were full fiscal years. Taking them at face value produced a leverage ratio of 41.67x from a five-week reporting period.

After (filtered to genuine full-year periods)

Coverage: undefined (correctly flagged)
Bausch Health reports D&A as two separate tags, not one

Pharma companies with large intangible assets often split "depreciation" and "amortization of intangibles" into separate XBRL tags rather than one combined figure — the single-tag search came up empty.

Party City's goodwill impairment was double-counted

A specific "goodwill impairment" tag and a generic "asset impairment" catch-all tag turned out to represent overlapping figures for the same year — summing both inflated the add-back to $1.43B against a real disclosed total of $862.5M.

SEC's own ticker file quietly excludes delisted companies

SEC's ticker-to-CIK lookup only covers currently actively-traded tickers — the moment a company delists (goes private, gets acquired, liquidates), its ticker vanishes from that file even though its CIK and filings remain in EDGAR permanently. Since almost every genuinely distressed company eventually delists, this hit the distressed group hardest: 11 of 12 new distressed candidates failed to resolve on the first attempt.

2 of 10 complete rows were distressed (badly imbalanced)
After (CIKs hardcoded directly, verified via EDGAR filing search)
6 of 14 complete rows were distressed
Charter's interest expense was tagged as a negative number

Some filers report the "InterestExpense" concept as negative (an income-statement sign convention), not a real negative interest cost. A strict > 0 check silently rejected a perfectly real figure, producing an unexplained "INCOMPLETE" result with no visible reason.

Before
Raw value: -$5,188,000,000 → rejected, no reason shown

A blindly-added "Other" tag produced an impossible 962x coverage ratio

Adding InterestExpenseOther as an equal alternative to the main interest expense tag let a tiny sub-component override the real total for one filer, since the code just picked whichever tag was most recently filed. No real company sustains 50x+ coverage — a plain sanity check catches this class of error even when the exact wrong tag can't be pinned down.
